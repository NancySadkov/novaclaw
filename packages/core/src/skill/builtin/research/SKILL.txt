# Research — commandments for an agent doing research

For any agent — frontier or small — that measures something and reports what it found. Every rule
below was paid for by a real defect. Order is roughly the order in which they bite.

**The single sentence:** *a result you cannot explain is not a result, and a measurement you have not
attacked is not evidence.*

---

## I. Before you measure

**1. Check the instrument before you trust the number.** Verify what *produces* the result, not only
what surrounds it. Verifying the plumbing, the wiring and the parsing while assuming the SCORER is
the classic miss — everything around the measurement is examined and the measurement itself is taken
on faith.

**2. A scorer reads only what the SUBJECT PRODUCED.** Never what it wrote down. If the haystack
includes the subject's own files, notes, plans or arguments, it can pass by *asserting* the answer
instead of producing it. Score outputs, exit codes, artifacts the subject had to *build*.

**3. Demand a second, independent fact.** One string match is not a verdict. A good gate requires two
things that a shortcut cannot satisfy at once — *the expected output* **and** *a compiled binary*;
*the claim* **and** *the receipt*. The cross-check, not the match, is what makes it a measurement.

**4. 🔴 A control proves only the HALF of the harness it exercises — and by passing, makes the other
half look measured.** If the round trip is *send* then *receive*, a control that only reads proves
nothing about writing; every "zero results" is then a query for a record that was never stored, and
it will read as a finding about the subject. Ask which half yours covers and build one for the other.
Where an instrument outside your own code can answer the same question, use it, and validate it on a
known-positive in the same call.

**5. Failure and zero must not share a representation.** If a crashed run, an unparsed report and a
genuine zero all read as `0`, your pipeline will eventually report one as the other — and it will do
so as a *finding*, with no error attached. Give "we could not measure" its own value and its own
message. ⚠️ **The same applies to a CONDITION that cannot be evaluated.** A guard written
`if (found.length > 0 && !found.includes(x)) fail()` passes silently the day the parse returns
nothing: it reports success while checking nothing. A check that cannot locate its data must FAIL,
naming what it could not find — never skip.

**6. Write down how you will read the result BEFORE the data exists.** Which statistic, what counts
as an effect, what you will do with a null. A metric chosen after seeing which one separates is not a
measurement, it is a search. Pre-registration costs nothing when the answer is unknown and is
worthless afterwards.

**7. Predeclare what each possible outcome would LICENSE — and what it would not.** Especially the
outcome you expect. Doing this while the answer is unknown is the only time it is honest.

**8. 🔴 Control the ENVIRONMENT, not just the variable.** Everything that differs between arms is part
of the experiment, whether you chose it or not. Three that bite hardest:

- **The subject's own leftovers.** If run N can write where run N+1 reads — a scratch file, a cache, a
  notes file, an answer it produced last time — then the later runs faced an easier task and you have
  measured two different problems. Rebuild the world per arm, and *report* what the subject left
  behind rather than silently sweeping it: an artifact it invented is evidence about how it works.
- **Shared durable state.** A store, an index or a memory the subject can read across runs turns
  recall into apparent competence. Assert the precondition (empty, fresh, isolated) — do not assume
  it. A property that happens to hold today is not a control.
- **Ambient load.** A timing taken while the machine is contended measures the machine. Record the
  conditions with the number, and re-run when the box is quiet before believing a duration.

⚠️ And a limit YOU imposed — a deadline, a cap, a truncation — is part of the instrument. A run cut
off at your cap has told you it was slow and nothing else; do not let it enter the table beside runs
that finished.

## II. While you measure

**9. Change one thing.** If you must change two, you have two experiments, not one.

**10. Never fix the instrument mid-run without restarting the comparison.** A budget, a seed set or a
scorer changed halfway makes the completed arms incomparable with the rest. A known limitation
honestly stated beats a silently contaminated dataset.

**11. Keep every failure.** A run that crashed is a measurement about that configuration. Dropping it
biases everything downstream, and the summary must report the failed count rather than averaging it
away.

**12. Write results incrementally.** After every unit, never at the end. Long runs meet reboots,
timeouts and your own mistakes; a run that loses six hours to a crash lost them to the rig, not the
crash.

**13. Watch the SUBJECT, not the artifact.** A results file that stops growing looks identical
whether the run is slow or dead. Check liveness, check the server's own counters, check process
state. And a kill that reports failure has told you something — a kill that reports nothing has not.

---

## III. When you read the result

**14. 🔴 An implausible result is a bug report about your instrument.** This is the most important
rule here, and the easiest to skip because the number is *good*. If a subject that cannot do the easy
thing appears to have done the hard thing, stop and explain the gap before reporting it. *Do not wait
to be asked.* Suspicion is not something a reader supplies; it is the work.

**15. ⭐ An impossible DIRECTION is the cheapest instrument check you own.** A cumulative count that
decreases, a total that shrinks, a coverage that goes down, a monotone series that turns — none of
these can be the subject. They are the rig, announcing itself. Assert monotonicity wherever the
quantity should have it, so the rig says so instead of you noticing later. This costs one line and
catches a whole class of contaminated data before it becomes a published table.

**16. A VIEW is not a RECORD.** Endpoints, logs, consoles and UIs routinely hand you a truncated,
paginated, newest-first or actively REWRITTEN slice of history — often with no total, no cursor, and
nothing to say anything was dropped. Re-reading such a view as though it were the full record
produces an undercount of unknown size that looks exactly like data. Two defences, and you need both:
ask for the largest window the source permits, and **accumulate** — union each reading into a set
keyed on a stable id, so nothing you have already seen can be taken away. Prefer an append-only
source when one exists, and confirm it actually delivers before depending on it.

**17. Explain the mechanism, or say you cannot.** "It scored higher" is not a finding. *Why* — and if
you do not know, that sentence goes in the report.

**18. Correlation inside a transcript is not cause.** What a successful run *did* is not what made it
succeed; a run already going well produces different behaviour as a *symptom*. Test it as a cause or
label it as a guess.

**19. Count something that can only exist if the thing you are testing is present.** A mention that
survives the component's absence proves nothing — it can rank the arms backwards, because a failing
run often prints *more* of the surrounding text.

**20. Beware the number that arrives at exactly the boundary.** A score landing precisely at what the
subject could produce from memory, at a cap, at a default — treat coincidence with the boundary as
evidence of the boundary, not of the subject.

**21. One rate that sums two failure modes hides both.** "Chose the wrong tool" and "never tried"
scored as the same zero; "gave up" and "was still working" counted as the same incomplete. Before
tuning a stubbornly mediocre number, ask which distinct OUTCOMES it is adding together, split them,
and report the un-correctable case beside the rate rather than folded into its denominator. The split
frequently turns an apparent weakness into a different and larger claim.

**22. A/B before owning or disowning a failure.** Against the pre-change state, one variable at a
time. Do not adopt a defect you did not cause, and do not blame the platform for one you did.

**23. Widen the scope before concluding.** The checks you chose are the ones aimed at what you
*believe* you changed. Run the full gate; the interesting failures live outside that belief.

---

## IV. When you report

**24. Report nulls and refutations with the weight you would have given a hit.** A prediction you
made and refuted is a result. Delete nothing because it became inconvenient — a record of how a
plausible story failed is worth more than a record of only the ones that held.

**25. State what the result does NOT license.** Every design forecloses questions. Name them, or a
reader will assume you answered them.

**26. Distinguish "no detectable effect" from "no effect" from "harm."** If the differences sit
inside the noise floor, say so, and do not let a verdict column imply an ordering the data cannot
support.

**27. Give the distribution, not just the centre.** A median hides variance; a mean lets one sample
speak for all. When the spread IS the finding — the thing works sometimes rather than a little —
neither number can say so alone. ⚠️ Two clean samples in a row are not evidence the failure is gone;
consecutive successes are what a bimodal process looks like from one side.

**28. Say how many observations the claim rests on, and mean it.** Three runs where only one
discriminates is n=1. Report the effective n, not the nominal one.

**29. Retract loudly, specifically, and at the top.** Name every claim that falls and every claim
that stands, and say why each. A quiet correction is a second error. When the instrument was at
fault, say which numbers survive it and which do not — a reader cannot separate them for you.

**30. Put the finding where it is read first.** An appended document drifts: each edit locally
correct, the whole burying the point. A count in a heading is a claim like any other.

**31. Never park an answerable question.** "The owner should confirm" is a defect when the primary
source is two greps away. Escalate for credentials, hardware and permission — not for work.

---

## V. The posture

**32. You own correctness.** Assume no second reviewer. Report what you *observed*, not what should
be true, and when you are unsure, dig until you are or state plainly what is unverified.

**33. Prefer the check that can fail — then MAKE it fail.** A rule you can follow imperfectly needs a
check that fails loudly, not a better-worded rule. Build the file; do not grep it. Run it; do not
reason about it. ⭐ And a guard you have never seen red is a decoration: plant the condition it is
supposed to catch and watch it fire. Keep that negative control in the harness, so the day the guard
stops working something says so.

**34. Your own rig is the most likely confound.** More likely than the subject, and far more likely
than the platform. Suspect it first — and suspect it hardest when it is telling you what you hoped.
